14  Interpreting OLS: Outcome Weights

The companion chapter Interpreting OLS: Effect Weights read the OLS coefficient as a weighting of unobservable effects — it answered whose effect \(\hat\beta\) estimates. The same Frisch–Waugh–Lovell identity reads a second way, as a weighting of the observed outcomes. This reading answers a different question — which rows drive \(\hat\beta\), and in which direction — and, unlike the first, it is computable and generalises past OLS. This chapter follows it.

14.1 The other reading of FWL

Residualise the regressor of interest \(V\) on the controls \(X\), write \(\tilde V_i\) for the residual, and recall the residual-on-residual form of the coefficient, Equation 13.12:

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \tilde V_i y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n \tilde V_i^2}. \]

The previous chapter grouped each \(\tilde V_i\) with \(V_i\) and read \(\hat\beta\) as a weighting \(w_i = \tilde V_i V_i\) of effects (Equation 13.13). Group \(\tilde V_i\) with \(y_i\) instead — pairing each residual with its own outcome in the numerator — and the same expression is a linear combination of the observed outcomes:

\[ \hat\beta = \sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i y_i, \qquad \omega_i = \frac{\tilde V_i}{\sum_{j=1}^n \tilde V_j^2}. \tag{14.1}\]

Equation 14.1 is the same algebra rearranged, but a different object from the effect weights.

Two constraints pin \(\omega_i\) down, both from Equation 14.1. An intercept among the controls makes \(\sum_i \tilde V_i = 0\), so the weights sum to zero. And \(\sum_i \tilde V_i V_i = \sum_i \tilde V_i^2\) makes the weighted regressor sum to one, \(\sum_i \omega_i V_i = 1\).

Use this reading when overlap is in doubt and a row may enter with the wrong sign — which the non-negative \(w_i\) cannot show. Keep the effect reading for questions about the estimand.

14.2 What the outcome weights look like

Take the simple regression of \(y\) on \(x\) with an intercept, so \(\tilde V_i = x_i - \bar x = d_i\) and

\[ \omega_i = \frac{d_i}{\sum_j d_j^2}. \]

Unlike the effect weights \(w_i = d_i^2\) — non-negative, growing with the square of distance — this is linear in \(d_i\), and so signed: a point left of \(\bar x\) enters \(\hat\beta\) with a negative coefficient on its outcome, a point right of it positive. It is not a slope and does not involve \(\bar y\); it is simply a signed weight on the observed \(y_i\).

Simulation — simple regression. To see it, we simulate \(n = 100\) points — regressor \(x_i \sim N(5, 2^2)\) and outcome \(y_i = 2 x_i + \varepsilon_i\) with \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 3^2)\) — then size each point by \(|\omega_i|\) and colour it by sign.

set.seed(3)

Outcome weights in a 100-point sample. Each small point’s size scales with its weight \(|\omega_i|\), coloured by sign — teal (\(\omega_i > 0\)) pushes \(\hat\beta\) up, rust (\(\omega_i < 0\)) pulls it down; the dotted line is the fulcrum \(x = \bar x\). The two large dots are the \(|d|\)-weighted centroids of the points left and right of \(\bar x\); the solid segment joining them has slope \(\hat\beta\), and the dashed line is the OLS fit (same slope, shifted through the grand mean).

The printed sums confirm Equation 14.1: the weights split at \(\bar x\) into a positive mass (\(\approx +0.25\)) and an equal negative one (\(\approx -0.25\)), and \(\sum_i \omega_i x_i = 1\).

But the figure shows more. Split the points at \(\bar x\), weight each side by \(|d_i|\), and take its centroid — the two large dots. The segment joining them has slope \(\hat\beta\): the OLS slope is the rise over run between the low-\(x\) and high-\(x\) weighted centres.

The dashed fit shares that slope, shifted through the grand mean, so the two coincide only when the sides balance. This collapse to two points falls out of the slope formula.

14.2.1 Why it collapses to two points

Write \(d_i = x_i - \bar x\), so the slope is \(\hat\beta = \sum_i d_i y_i / \sum_i d_i^2\). Two elementary facts drive everything:

  • \(\sum_i d_i = 0\) — deviations from the mean sum to zero;
  • \(\sum_i d_i^2 = \sum_i d_i x_i\), since \(\sum_i d_i x_i = \sum_i d_i (d_i + \bar x) = \sum_i d_i^2 + \bar x \sum_i d_i\).

The second fact writes the slope as a ratio of two inner products with the same vector \(d\):

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{d \cdot y}{d \cdot x}. \]

Split the observations by the sign of \(d_i\): let \(R = \{i : d_i > 0\}\) lie right of the mean and \(L = \{i : d_i < 0\}\) left, with \(d_i = 0\) points contributing nothing. Let \(W = \sum_{i \in R} d_i = \sum_{i \in R} |d_i|\) be the total weight on the right. Because \(\sum_i d_i = 0\), the left must carry \(\sum_{i \in L} d_i = -W\), that is \(\sum_{i \in L} |d_i| = W\): both sides hold the identical total weight \(W\).

Weight each side by \(|d_i|\) and form its centroid, \(\bar v_R = \sum_{i \in R} |d_i| v_i / W\) and \(\bar v_L = \sum_{i \in L} |d_i| v_i / W\) for any variable \(v\). Using \(|d_i| = d_i\) on \(R\) and \(|d_i| = -d_i\) on \(L\),

\[ d \cdot v = \sum_{i \in R} d_i v_i + \sum_{i \in L} d_i v_i = W \bar v_R - W \bar v_L = W(\bar v_R - \bar v_L). \]

An inner product with a zero-sum vector is a between-group contrast — the common weight \(W\) times the gap between the two weighted side-means. Apply it to the numerator (\(v = y\)) and the denominator (\(v = x\)):

\[ \hat\beta = \frac{d \cdot y}{d \cdot x} = \frac{W(\bar y_R - \bar y_L)}{W(\bar x_R - \bar x_L)} = \frac{\bar y_R - \bar y_L}{\bar x_R - \bar x_L}. \]

The \(W\) cancels, and what remains is the plain rise over run between the two \(|d|\)-weighted centroids — the two dots in the figure.

The construction gives exactly two points because \(d_i\) takes two signs: a centred regressor has a left and a right of the mean, and the sign is constant within each side, so each half collapses to a single weighted centre.

The weight must be \(|d_i|\), not a plain average, because it already sits inside the OLS sums \(d \cdot y\) and \(d \cdot x\): the dot is a centre of mass with mass \(|d_i|\), and the same mass fixes both coordinates — weighting \(y\) but not \(x\) gives the wrong slope.

14.3 When the sign flips

In simple regression the sign of \(\omega_i\) is just the side of the mean: half the weights are negative, structurally, and a low-\(x\) point should count against a high-\(x\) one. With controls \(\tilde V_i\) becomes a residual, and a row can flip to the sign its raw position denies — a hidden extrapolation the non-negative \(w_i\) can never show.

Take a binary treatment \(D\) with covariate \(x\) and thin overlap — controls low, treated high — and fit \(y \sim D + x\). Now \(\omega_i \propto \tilde V_i = D_i - \hat D_i\), with \(\hat D\) the linear projection of \(D\) on \((1, x)\). Where overlap fails, that \(\hat D\) leaves \([0, 1]\): a treated unit with \(\hat D_i > 1\) gets \(\tilde V_i < 0\), so its outcome is subtracted from \(\hat\beta\) — a sign \(\tilde V_i^2 \ge 0\) hides.

Simulation — thin overlap. The DGP: \(n = 80\), with 40 controls at \(x \sim N(2, 0.7^2)\) and 40 treated at \(x \sim N(5, 0.7^2)\) — barely overlapping — and \(y_i = D_i + 0.6\,x_i + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 0.5^2)\), so the true effect is \(1\).

set.seed(11)
n0 <- 40; n1 <- 40
x <- c(rnorm(n0, 2.0, 0.7), rnorm(n1, 5.0, 0.7))
d <- c(rep(0, n0), rep(1, n1))
y <- 1.0 * d + 0.6 * x + rnorm(n0 + n1, 0, 0.5)

Seventeen of the forty treated units carry a negative weight, and \(\hat\beta\) still equals \(\sum_i \omega_i y_i\) exactly. Nothing is wrong with the algebra: asked to compare treated and control where they barely coexist, the linear model extrapolates, and the extrapolation surfaces as flipped signs.

Which negatives matter? Two kinds.

Structural: regressed on the treatment alone, every treated unit is positive and every control negative, and \(\hat\beta = \bar y_1 - \bar y_0\).

Wrong-sign: the opposite pairing — a treated unit with \(\omega_i < 0\), or a control with \(\omega_i > 0\). This cannot happen without covariates. It appears only when the residual \(\tilde V_i = D_i - \hat D_i\) carries a fitted \(\hat D_i\) outside \([0, 1]\), and the counts above confirm the treated units with \(\omega_i < 0\) are exactly those with \(\hat D_i > 1\).

So the test is one line: fit the treatment on the controls; any \(\hat D_i \notin [0, 1]\) is an extrapolated, wrong-sign unit — a point where treated and control covariates fail to overlap. (With a continuous regressor there is no bounded range to leave: a negative \(\omega_i\) just means “below the conditional mean”, and overlap is read from leverage, not sign.)

A wrong-sign weight is extrapolation: the model reaches outside common support, and \(\hat\beta\) can leave the convex hull of the observed outcomes — outside the range of any real comparison.

This is no failure of OLS; it locates the reliance on functional form. The responses are the usual ones: trim to common support (the negatives then vanish), interpolate instead (matching, or overlap weights that are non-negative by design), or report how far \(\hat\beta\) moves when the extrapolated units are dropped.

This is the implied-weights diagnostic of Chattopadhyay and Zubizarreta (2023); their lmw package computes the weights and the balance, extrapolation, and influence diagnostics built on them, and OutcomeWeights (Knaus 2024) carries it to machine-learning estimators. On the data above, lmw’s negative weights are exactly the units our \(\hat D \notin [0, 1]\) rule flags:

The mainstream overlap check takes the other route, through the propensity score. WeightIt estimates it and reports the effective sample size and weight range, cobalt::bal.plot draws the two groups’ score densities, and the fixes are trimming (Crump et al. 2009) or overlap weights, the "ATO" estimand (Li, Morgan, and Zaslavsky 2018).

On this data both routes agree — the logistic score perfectly separates:

w <- WeightIt::weightit(d ~ x, data = df, method = "glm", estimand = "ATE")

        `min treated PS` = min(w$ps[d == 1])), 3)

Both routes point to the same problem — the groups barely overlap — using different tools: the regression’s own implied weights (lmw), or a separate propensity-score model (WeightIt, cobalt). Here overlap fails completely, so neither can help; there is no shared region to weight toward.

When overlap is only partial, both diagnostics still work. The propensity-score route shows it through a shrinking effective sample size and a few large weights; the regression route, through a few negative weights. The next example is that milder case.

On data with partial overlap the diagnostic is a routine check: does the reported coefficient depend on the extrapolation? The answer can go either way, and trimming the flagged units and refitting is what settles it.

Here it depends, and by a lot. Suppose the outcome is convex in \(x\), with controls low on the curve and treated high, so the two groups sit on different parts of it. Fit the straight \(y \sim D + x\) regardless and it reports a treatment gap of \(\hat\beta = 0.69\), a third short of the true effect of \(1\) — an artifact of one line forced through a curve the groups do not share.

Simulation — fragile estimate under misspecification. The DGP: 150 controls near \(x = 2.5\) and 150 treated near \(x = 4.5\) (both sd \(1.1\)), with \(y_i = D_i + 0.8\,(x_i - 3.5)^2 + 0.3\,x_i + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 0.5^2)\) — a constant true effect of \(1\) sitting on a convex baseline.

set.seed(7)
n <- 150

A fragile OLS gap. The two curves are the true means — control (rust, solid) and treated (teal, dotted), a constant \(1\) apart: that gap is the true effect (arrow). Controls sit low on the curve and treated high, so the groups share only the middle. Ringed points are the extrapolating units the one-line rule flags, where the groups do not overlap. Forcing a straight \(y \sim D + x\) across the whole range reports \(\hat\beta = 0.69\); dropping the flagged units, or reweighting to the overlap, restores \(\approx 1\).

The outcome weights find the artifact. Thirty-seven units carry the wrong sign, all in the non-overlapping tails. Trim them and refit and \(\hat\beta\) returns to \(0.99\); reweight to the overlap instead — the "ATO" weights — and it returns \(0.98\). Either way the coefficient had leaned on the extrapolation, and the diagnostic showed it, read off the regression itself with no separate model.

Whether it matters depends on the data. Here trimming moved \(\hat\beta\) a lot, so the estimate leaned on the extrapolation. Had the outcome been linear — the two groups sharing one straight line — the same trim would barely move \(\hat\beta\): the negative weights are still there, but dropping those units changes almost nothing. The check tells you which case you are in.

And it survives where the propensity score cannot. On the perfectly-separated data above the logistic model fails to converge and inverse-probability weighting collapses, yet the implied weights still flag every extrapolating unit.

14.4 Out of OLS: the same weights beyond the linear model

The outcome reading has one advantage the effect reading lacks: it survives leaving OLS. Any estimator that can be written \(\hat\tau = \sum_i \omega_i Y_i = \boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y\) — a linear combination of the observed outcomes — has outcome weights, whatever machinery produced them.

Knaus (2024) makes this the organising idea: augmented inverse-probability weighting, double machine learning, and the generalized random forest all admit a closed-form \(\boldsymbol\omega\), obtained by the same residual-on-residual logic as OLS and computed off the shelf by his OutcomeWeights package. We derive it below; first, what it shows.

Simulation — heterogeneous effects (DML). Under a heterogeneous effect the two estimators diverge, and the weights show why. We simulate \(n = 2000\) units: five standard-normal covariates \(X\), selection \(W_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\Lambda(-1 + 1.2\,X_{i1}))\) with \(\Lambda\) the logistic function (treatment is scarce and tied to \(X_1\)), and outcome \(Y_i = \tau_i W_i + X_{i1} + 0.5\,X_{i2} + \varepsilon_i\) with an effect \(\tau_i = 1 + X_{i1}\) that varies across units — a \(W \times X_1\) interaction. The average effect is \(E[\tau] = 1\), but units with larger \(X_1\) have larger effects. dml_with_smoother returns a partially-linear (PLR) and an AIPW estimator; get_outcome_weights extracts the weight matrix \(\boldsymbol\omega\), one row per estimator.

library(OutcomeWeights)
set.seed(1)
n <- 2000; p <- 5
X <- matrix(rnorm(n * p), n, p)
etrue <- plogis(-1 + 1.2 * X[, 1])                       # asymmetric selection on X1
W <- rbinom(n, 1, etrue)
tau <- 1 + X[, 1]                                        # heterogeneous effect: W x X1
Y <- tau * W + X[, 1] + 0.5 * X[, 2] + rnorm(n)
dml <- dml_with_smoother(Y, W, X, estimators = c("PLR", "AIPW_ATE"))
om  <- get_outcome_weights(dml)                          # om$omega: 2 x n, om$treat = W
invisible(capture.output(s <- summary(dml)))
W1 <- om$treat == 1
tab <- t(sapply(rownames(om$omega), function(nm) {
  w <- om$omega[nm, ]
  c(estimate = s[nm, 1], `omega %*% Y` = sum(w * Y),
    treated = sum(w[W1]), control = sum(w[!W1]))
}))
ov <- etrue * (1 - etrue)                                # e(1-e): PLR's per-unit weight
# ATE = plain mean of tau; overlap-ATE = e(1-e)-weighted mean of tau (PLR's target)
cat(sprintf("true ATE = %.2f   true overlap-ATE = %.2f\n", mean(tau), sum(ov * tau) / sum(ov)))

mtab(tab, 3)

AIPW-ATE 1.074 1.074 1.000 -1.000

Two things stand out. First, the estimators now disagree — PLR reports \(1.29\), AIPW \(1.07\) — and neither is wrong; they target different populations. PLR weights each unit by \(e(X)(1 - e(X))\), the conditional variance of treatment — Angrist’s propensity-variance weight from the previous chapter — so it leans on units where treatment is near a coin flip; its estimand is that \(e(1-e)\)-weighted average of the unit effects (\(\approx 1.3\)). AIPW instead reweights by \(1/e\) and \(1/(1-e)\) to undo selection, returning the plain average, the ATE (\(\approx 1\)). Same outcomes \(\boldsymbol Y\), weighted two ways, two population-averages of \(\tau\) — and the weights are what make the difference visible.

Second, the weights still reproduce each estimate exactly and sum to zero overall. They part on the other constraint from §1, \(\sum_i \omega_i V_i = 1\): for a binary treatment that constraint is the treated weights summing to \(+1\) (and, with the zero total, controls to \(-1\)). AIPW keeps it exactly; the partially-linear estimator sums to \(\pm 0.99\), quietly breaking the within-group budget that OLS and AIPW hold.

That divergence shows what the representation is for. In Knaus’s (2024) framing it does three things beyond producing the estimate. It puts OLS, IPW, AIPW, DML, and the causal forest on one footing, comparable through the single object \(\boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y\). It characterises the target population each estimator reports — exactly the PLR-versus-AIPW gap above, two weightings of one set of outcomes. And it checks covariate balance, by handing \(\boldsymbol\omega\) to cobalt, and flags when weights fail a property usually assumed — the within-group sum-to-\(\pm 1\) that PLR breaks here.

The signed-extrapolation and leverage reading of §3 carries over too, but that is the lmw lineage the representation shares, not Knaus’s own emphasis.

14.4.1 The weights, derived

We have the OLS weights (Equation 14.1); the same weights exist for the machine-learning estimators, derived the same way — residualise, then read off the coefficient on \(\boldsymbol Y\). Take the partially-linear estimator (the R-learner behind the causal forest). It is FWL with the projections done by a flexible learner: residualise treatment and outcome on the covariates, then regress one residual on the other,

\[ \hat\tau = \frac{\sum_i (D_i - \hat e_i)(Y_i - \hat m_i)}{\sum_i (D_i - \hat e_i)^2}, \]

with \(\hat e = \hat E[D \mid X]\) and \(\hat m = \hat E[Y \mid X]\) the fitted nuisances.

The outcome nuisance is a smoother: its fitted values are a linear map of the training outcomes, \(\hat{\boldsymbol m} = \boldsymbol S \boldsymbol Y\) — true for OLS, ridge, kernels, and honest forests alike. So the outcome residual is \(\boldsymbol Y - \hat{\boldsymbol m} = (\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S)\boldsymbol Y\). Writing \(\tilde{\boldsymbol D} = \boldsymbol D - \hat{\boldsymbol e}\) for the treatment residual and substituting,

\[ \hat\tau = \frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol D}'(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S)\,\boldsymbol Y}{\tilde{\boldsymbol D}'\tilde{\boldsymbol D}}. \]

Now \(\boldsymbol Y\) appears once, linearly, in the numerator. Read off its coefficient and the weights are done:

\[ \hat\tau = \boldsymbol\omega'\boldsymbol Y, \qquad \boldsymbol\omega' = \frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol D}'(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S)}{\tilde{\boldsymbol D}'\tilde{\boldsymbol D}}. \tag{14.2}\]

That is Equation 14.1 — the OLS weights — with the projection annihilator \(\boldsymbol M_X\) replaced by the smoother annihilator \(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S\). OLS is the case of a linear learner, \(\boldsymbol S = \boldsymbol P_X\): there \(\boldsymbol S\) is a projection, \(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S = \boldsymbol M_X\), and Equation 14.2 is Equation 14.1 exactly.

Three moves did it: write the estimator, substitute the smoother \(\hat{\boldsymbol m} = \boldsymbol S \boldsymbol Y\), read off the coefficient on \(\boldsymbol Y\). They work for any estimator built from smoothers; only the operator on \(\boldsymbol Y\) changes. AIPW is worth carrying through, because its operator is more than \(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S\).

AIPW. With outcome smoothers \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1 = \boldsymbol S_1 \boldsymbol Y\), \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_0 = \boldsymbol S_0 \boldsymbol Y\) and propensity \(\hat{\boldsymbol e}\), the score of unit \(i\) is

\[ \psi_i = \hat\mu_{1i} - \hat\mu_{0i} + \frac{D_i}{\hat e_i}\,(Y_i - \hat\mu_{1i}) - \frac{1 - D_i}{1 - \hat e_i}\,(Y_i - \hat\mu_{0i}), \qquad \hat\tau = \frac1n \sum_i \psi_i. \]

Each \(\hat\mu_{ji}\) is \((\boldsymbol S_j \boldsymbol Y)_i\), so \(\boldsymbol Y\) again enters only linearly; collecting its coefficient gives \(\hat\tau = \boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y\) with

\[ \boldsymbol T = \boldsymbol S_1 - \boldsymbol S_0 + \operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\tfrac{\boldsymbol D}{\hat{\boldsymbol e}}\Big)(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S_1) - \operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\tfrac{1 - \boldsymbol D}{1 - \hat{\boldsymbol e}}\Big)(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S_0), \qquad \boldsymbol\omega' = \frac{\mathbf 1' \boldsymbol T}{n}. \]

These derived weights are exactly the rows of the table above. Multiply either \(\boldsymbol\omega\) into \(\boldsymbol Y\) and you recover that estimator’s point estimate; sum them within the treated and control groups and you get the \(\pm 1\) (AIPW) and \(\pm 0.99\) (PLR) reported there. That is the use of deriving \(\boldsymbol\omega\): once you have it, the reproduction check, the within-group budget, and the balance and overlap diagnostics all run on the same object, for any estimator built from smoothers.

The package does exactly this — reads \((\boldsymbol S, \boldsymbol S_0, \boldsymbol S_1, \hat{\boldsymbol e})\) from the fitted object, forms these operators, and reads off \(\boldsymbol\omega\). The formulas above take a few lines to compute by hand; here we do it for both PLR and AIPW and check they match the package’s weights:

14.4.2 Two uses: balance and extrapolation

Having \(\boldsymbol\omega\), two checks run directly on it. Does the estimator’s implied weighting balance the covariates? Hand \(\boldsymbol\omega\) to cobalt and read the largest standardised mean difference. And is it extrapolating? 14.5 From two dots to four

The two-dot construction scales with how saturated the design is: each cell the regressors carve out collapses to its mean, and \(\hat\beta\) is a signed contrast of those cell means. Two cases make it concrete.

Simulation — treatment alone. Regress \(y\) on the treatment \(D\) alone — here 30 treated drawn \(y \sim N(5, 1)\) and 50 controls \(y \sim N(2, 1)\). The residual \(\tilde V_i = D_i - \bar D\) is constant within each group, so the \(|d|\)-weighting is uniform there and the two dots are simply the group means: \(\hat\beta = \bar y_1 - \bar y_0\), the difference in means. Every treated unit then carries positive weight, every control negative — the structural pattern of the previous section, with the wrong-sign off-diagonal empty until covariates enter.

Simulation — 2×2 difference-in-differences. Add a period and interact it with the group, and the design has four cells. Each collapses to its mean, and the interaction coefficient is their double contrast,

\[ \widehat{\text{DiD}} = (\bar y_{T,\text{post}} - \bar y_{T,\text{pre}}) - (\bar y_{C,\text{post}} - \bar y_{C,\text{pre}}). \]

The clean \(2 \times 2\) has trivial weights — four cell means, each \(\pm 1\). Real difference-in-differences carries covariates: parallel trends holds only conditional on \(X\), and the comparison has to be modelled. That model is a weighting of outcomes too.

The doubly-robust DiD of Sant’Anna and Zhao (2020) is AIPW read on the outcome change \(\Delta Y = Y_1 - Y_0\): a propensity for treatment, a control-group regression of \(\Delta Y\) on \(X\), combined. By the recipe of the last section its estimate is \(\widehat{\text{ATT}} = \boldsymbol\omega' \Delta Y\), with \(\boldsymbol\omega' = \boldsymbol a'(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S)\) — the control-trend smoother \(\boldsymbol S\) and the group weights \(\boldsymbol a = \boldsymbol w_{\text{treat}} - \boldsymbol w_{\text{cont}}\).

Simulation — doubly-robust DiD. Two periods and \(n = 2000\): treatment is selected on \(x_1\) with thin overlap, \(\Pr(D = 1) = \Lambda(-0.5 + 2 x_1)\); the control trend depends on \(x_1\), so trends are parallel only given \(X\); the true ATT is \(1\). We fit DR-DiD with DRDID, rebuild its weights by hand, and check.

library(DRDID)
set.seed(2)
n  <- 2000
x1 <- rnorm(n); x2 <- rnorm(n); Xcov <- cbind(1, x1, x2)
D  <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.5 + 2 * x1))        # selection into treatment on x1 (thin overlap)
y0 <- 1 + x1 + 0.5 * x2 + rnorm(n)               # pre-period outcome
y1 <- y0 + (0.5 + 0.4 * x1) + 1 * D + rnorm(n)   # control trend depends on x1; true ATT = 1
dY <- y1 - y0
att <- drdid_panel(y1 = y1, y0 = y0, D = D, covariates = Xcov, boot = FALSE)$ATT

# rebuild the DR-DiD weights: omega = (I - S)' a, acting on the outcome change dY
e    <- pmin(as.vector(glm(D ~ x1 + x2, family = binomial)$fitted), 1 - 1e-6)
keep <- rep(TRUE, n); keep[D == 0] <- e[D == 0] < 0.995     # DRDID's control trimming
Xc   <- Xcov[D == 0, ]
S    <- matrix(0, n, n); S[, D == 0] <- Xcov %*% solve(crossprod(Xc)) %*% t(Xc)
w.t  <- keep * D
w.c  <- keep * e * (1 - D) / (1 - e)
a    <- w.t / sum(w.t) - w.c / sum(w.c)          # group weights, treated minus reweighted control
omega <- as.vector(a - crossprod(S, a))

Xk <- cbind(x1, x2); sds <- apply(Xk, 2, sd)
vtab(c(`DRDID ATT`                = att,
       `omega' dY`                = sum(omega * dY),
       `wrong-sign weights`       = sum((D == 1 & omega < 0) | (D == 0 & omega > 0)),

\(\boldsymbol\omega' \Delta Y\) reproduces the DRDID estimate exactly — the difference-in-differences is a signed weighting of observed outcome changes, and the diagnostics of this chapter apply to it. The weighting balances the covariate that drove selection (its standardised imbalance falls from \(1.20\) to \(0.09\)). And \(151\) units carry a wrong-sign weight — a treated unit subtracted, or a control added — the §3 signature of a comparison reaching past common support.

Negative weights return in staggered designs one level up — among the comparisons rather than the units. Two-way fixed-effects DiD averages many such \(2 \times 2\)’s, and some enter with a negative weight: the forbidden comparisons that use already-treated units as controls (de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2020; Goodman-Bacon 2021). Those weight the treatment effects, not the outcomes — the effect-weight cousin — but the reading is the one just seen: a comparison entering with a sign it should not, because the design has run out of clean controls.

A closing caution. These weights diagnose estimation — which comparisons a difference-in-differences rests on, and whether it leans on non-overlapping or forbidden ones — not identification.

Parallel trends is an assumption about the treated group’s unobserved untreated path, and no weighting of observed outcomes can test it: a negative weight and a failure of parallel trends are independent, either able to occur without the other.

Pre-trends are probed separately, by the event-study leads. And weights chosen to match the pre-treatment paths — synthetic difference-in-differences and its kin — are a different object from the ones implied here.

14.6 Summary

  1. The second reading. FWL writes \(\hat\beta = \sum_i \omega_i y_i\) with \(\omega_i = \tilde V_i / \sum_j \tilde V_j^2\) (Equation 14.1): a signed, computable weighting of the observed outcomes, distinct from the effect weights \(w_i = \tilde V_i V_i\).
  2. Two constraints. The outcome weights sum to zero and the weighted regressor to one: \(\sum_i \omega_i = 0\), \(\sum_i \omega_i V_i = 1\).
  3. Signed, not squared. Because \(\omega_i \propto \tilde V_i\) rather than \(\tilde V_i^2\), it reveals direction: which rows push \(\hat\beta\) up, which down, and — under controls with thin overlap — which enter with a sign their raw position would not predict.
  4. It leaves OLS. Any \(\hat\tau = \boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y\) has outcome weights; the same object and its diagnostics extend to AIPW, DML, and causal forests (Knaus 2024), with the caveat that the tidy sum-to-zero of OLS need not become sum-to-\(\pm 1\) within groups.
  5. Saturation scales it. Regressed on the treatment alone, \(\hat\beta\) is the difference in group means — two dots; a \(2 \times 2\) difference-in-differences is a signed contrast of four cell means — four dots. With covariates a doubly-robust DiD is a weighting of the outcome changes, \(\widehat{\text{ATT}} = \boldsymbol\omega' \Delta Y\) (Sant’Anna and Zhao 2020), open to the same diagnostics; negative weights return in staggered designs as forbidden comparisons (de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2020).

The companion chapter [Interpreting OLS: Effect Weights](interpreting-ols.qmd) read the OLS coefficient as a weighting of unobservable effects — it answered *whose* effect $\hat\beta$ estimates. The same Frisch–Waugh–Lovell identity reads a second way, as a weighting of the *observed* outcomes. This reading answers a different question — which rows drive $\hat\beta$, and in which direction — and, unlike the first, it is computable and generalises past OLS. This chapter follows it.

## The other reading of FWL

Residualise the regressor of interest $V$ on the controls $X$, write $\tilde V_i$ for the residual, and recall the residual-on-residual form of the coefficient, @eq-fwl:

$$ \hat\beta = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \tilde V_i y_i}{\sum_{i=1}^n \tilde V_i^2}. $$

The previous chapter grouped each $\tilde V_i$ with $V_i$ and read $\hat\beta$ as a weighting $w_i = \tilde V_i V_i$ of effects (@eq-weff). Group $\tilde V_i$ with $y_i$ instead — pairing each residual with its own outcome in the numerator — and the *same* expression is a linear combination of the observed outcomes:

$$ \hat\beta = \sum_{i=1}^n \omega_i y_i, \qquad \omega_i = \frac{\tilde V_i}{\sum_{j=1}^n \tilde V_j^2}. $$ {#eq-omega}

@eq-omega is the same algebra rearranged, but a different object from the effect weights.

| | Weights on outcomes $\omega_i$ | Weights on effects $w_i$ |
|---|---|---|
| Weights what | the observed $y_i$ | that observation's own effect $\beta_i$ |
| Sign | signed, sums to zero | non-negative (under saturation) |
| Computable | yes, $y$ is observed | no, the $\beta_i$ are never seen |
| Holds when | always, it is algebra | in expectation; per-unit needs saturation |
| Answers | which rows drive $\hat\beta$, any wrong sign | is this the effect I meant, whose effect |

Two constraints pin $\omega_i$ down, both from @eq-omega. An intercept among the controls makes $\sum_i \tilde V_i = 0$, so the weights **sum to zero**. And $\sum_i \tilde V_i V_i = \sum_i \tilde V_i^2$ makes the weighted regressor **sum to one**, $\sum_i \omega_i V_i = 1$.

Use this reading when overlap is in doubt and a row may enter with the wrong sign — which the non-negative $w_i$ cannot show. Keep the effect reading for questions about the estimand.

## What the outcome weights look like

Take the simple regression of $y$ on $x$ with an intercept, so $\tilde V_i = x_i - \bar x = d_i$ and

$$ \omega_i = \frac{d_i}{\sum_j d_j^2}. $$

Unlike the effect weights $w_i = d_i^2$ — non-negative, growing with the *square* of distance — this is **linear in $d_i$, and so signed**: a point left of $\bar x$ enters $\hat\beta$ with a negative coefficient on its outcome, a point right of it positive. It is not a slope and does not involve $\bar y$; it is simply a signed weight on the observed $y_i$.

**Simulation — simple regression.** To see it, we simulate $n = 100$ points — regressor $x_i \sim N(5, 2^2)$ and outcome $y_i = 2 x_i + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 3^2)$ — then size each point by $|\omega_i|$ and colour it by sign.

But the figure shows more. Split the points at $\bar x$, weight each side by $|d_i|$, and take its centroid — the two large dots. **The segment joining them has slope $\hat\beta$:** the OLS slope is the rise over run between the low-$x$ and high-$x$ weighted centres.

The dashed fit shares that slope, shifted through the grand mean, so the two coincide only when the sides balance. This collapse to two points falls out of the slope formula.

### Why it collapses to two points

Write $d_i = x_i - \bar x$, so the slope is $\hat\beta = \sum_i d_i y_i / \sum_i d_i^2$. Two elementary facts drive everything:

- $\sum_i d_i = 0$ — deviations from the mean sum to zero;
- $\sum_i d_i^2 = \sum_i d_i x_i$, since $\sum_i d_i x_i = \sum_i d_i (d_i + \bar x) = \sum_i d_i^2 + \bar x \sum_i d_i$.

The second fact writes the slope as a ratio of two inner products with the *same* vector $d$:

$$ \hat\beta = \frac{d \cdot y}{d \cdot x}. $$

Split the observations by the sign of $d_i$: let $R = \{i : d_i > 0\}$ lie right of the mean and $L = \{i : d_i < 0\}$ left, with $d_i = 0$ points contributing nothing. Let $W = \sum_{i \in R} d_i = \sum_{i \in R} |d_i|$ be the total weight on the right. Because $\sum_i d_i = 0$, the left must carry $\sum_{i \in L} d_i = -W$, that is $\sum_{i \in L} |d_i| = W$: **both sides hold the identical total weight $W$.**

Weight each side by $|d_i|$ and form its centroid, $\bar v_R = \sum_{i \in R} |d_i| v_i / W$ and $\bar v_L = \sum_{i \in L} |d_i| v_i / W$ for any variable $v$. Using $|d_i| = d_i$ on $R$ and $|d_i| = -d_i$ on $L$,

$$ d \cdot v = \sum_{i \in R} d_i v_i + \sum_{i \in L} d_i v_i = W \bar v_R - W \bar v_L = W(\bar v_R - \bar v_L). $$

An inner product with a zero-sum vector is a *between-group contrast* — the common weight $W$ times the gap between the two weighted side-means. Apply it to the numerator ($v = y$) and the denominator ($v = x$):

$$ \hat\beta = \frac{d \cdot y}{d \cdot x} = \frac{W(\bar y_R - \bar y_L)}{W(\bar x_R - \bar x_L)} = \frac{\bar y_R - \bar y_L}{\bar x_R - \bar x_L}. $$

The $W$ cancels, and what remains is the plain rise over run between the two $|d|$-weighted centroids — the two dots in the figure.

The construction gives exactly *two* points because $d_i$ takes two signs: a centred regressor has a left and a right of the mean, and the sign is constant within each side, so each half collapses to a single weighted centre.

The weight must be $|d_i|$, not a plain average, because it already sits inside the OLS sums $d \cdot y$ and $d \cdot x$: the dot is a centre of mass with mass $|d_i|$, and the same mass fixes both coordinates — weighting $y$ but not $x$ gives the wrong slope.

## When the sign flips

In simple regression the sign of $\omega_i$ is just the side of the mean: half the weights are negative, structurally, and a low-$x$ point *should* count against a high-$x$ one. With controls $\tilde V_i$ becomes a *residual*, and a row can flip to the sign its raw position denies — a hidden extrapolation the non-negative $w_i$ can never show.

Take a binary treatment $D$ with covariate $x$ and thin overlap — controls low, treated high — and fit $y \sim D + x$. Now $\omega_i \propto \tilde V_i = D_i - \hat D_i$, with $\hat D$ the *linear* projection of $D$ on $(1, x)$. Where overlap fails, that $\hat D$ leaves $[0, 1]$: a treated unit with $\hat D_i > 1$ gets $\tilde V_i < 0$, so its outcome is *subtracted* from $\hat\beta$ — a sign $\tilde V_i^2 \ge 0$ hides.

**Simulation — thin overlap.** The DGP: $n = 80$, with 40 controls at $x \sim N(2, 0.7^2)$ and 40 treated at $x \sim N(5, 0.7^2)$ — barely overlapping — and $y_i = D_i + 0.6\,x_i + \varepsilon_i$, $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 0.5^2)$, so the true effect is $1$.

```{r}
#| fig-width: 9
#| fig-height: 4.2

set.seed(11)
```

Seventeen of the forty treated units carry a negative weight, and $\hat\beta$ still equals $\sum_i \omega_i y_i$ exactly. Nothing is wrong with the algebra: asked to compare treated and control where they barely coexist, the linear model extrapolates, and the extrapolation surfaces as flipped signs.

Which negatives matter? Two kinds.

*Structural:* regressed on the treatment alone, every treated unit is positive and every control negative, and $\hat\beta = \bar y_1 - \bar y_0$.

*Wrong-sign:* the opposite pairing — a treated unit with $\omega_i < 0$, or a control with $\omega_i > 0$. This cannot happen without covariates. It appears only when the residual $\tilde V_i = D_i - \hat D_i$ carries a fitted $\hat D_i$ outside $[0, 1]$, and the counts above confirm the treated units with $\omega_i < 0$ are exactly those with $\hat D_i > 1$.

So the test is one line: **fit the treatment on the controls; any $\hat D_i \notin [0, 1]$ is an extrapolated, wrong-sign unit** — a point where treated and control covariates fail to overlap. (With a continuous regressor there is no bounded range to leave: a negative $\omega_i$ just means "below the conditional mean", and overlap is read from leverage, not sign.)

A wrong-sign weight is extrapolation: the model reaches outside common support, and $\hat\beta$ can leave the convex hull of the observed outcomes — outside the range of any real comparison.

This is no failure of OLS; it locates the reliance on functional form. The responses are the usual ones: trim to common support (the negatives then vanish), interpolate instead (matching, or overlap weights that are non-negative by design), or report how far $\hat\beta$ moves when the extrapolated units are dropped.

This is the implied-weights diagnostic of Chattopadhyay and Zubizarreta (2023); their `lmw` package computes the weights and the balance, extrapolation, and influence diagnostics built on them, and `OutcomeWeights` (Knaus 2024) carries it to machine-learning estimators. On the data above, `lmw`'s negative weights are exactly the units our $\hat D \notin [0, 1]$ rule flags:

```{r}
knitr::kable(

```

The mainstream overlap check takes the other route, through the propensity score. `WeightIt` estimates it and reports the effective sample size and weight range, `cobalt::bal.plot` draws the two groups' score densities, and the fixes are trimming (Crump et al. 2009) or overlap weights, the `"ATO"` estimand (Li, Morgan, and Zaslavsky 2018).

On this data both routes agree — the logistic score *perfectly separates*:

```{r}

```

Both routes point to the same problem — the groups barely overlap — using different tools: the regression's own implied weights (`lmw`), or a separate propensity-score model (`WeightIt`, `cobalt`). Here overlap fails completely, so neither can help; there is no shared region to weight toward.

When overlap is only partial, both diagnostics still work. The propensity-score route shows it through a shrinking effective sample size and a few large weights; the regression route, through a few negative weights. The next example is that milder case.

On data with partial overlap the diagnostic is a routine check: does the reported coefficient depend on the extrapolation? The answer can go either way, and trimming the flagged units and refitting is what settles it.

Here it depends, and by a lot. Suppose the outcome is convex in $x$, with controls low on the curve and treated high, so the two groups sit on different parts of it. Fit the straight $y \sim D + x$ regardless and it reports a treatment gap of $\hat\beta = 0.69$, a third short of the true effect of $1$ — an artifact of one line forced through a curve the groups do not share.

**Simulation — fragile estimate under misspecification.** The DGP: 150 controls near $x = 2.5$ and 150 treated near $x = 4.5$ (both sd $1.1$), with $y_i = D_i + 0.8\,(x_i - 3.5)^2 + 0.3\,x_i + \varepsilon_i$, $\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 0.5^2)$ — a constant true effect of $1$ sitting on a convex baseline.

```{r}
#| fig-width: 6.2
#| fig-height: 4.6
```

The outcome weights find the artifact. Thirty-seven units carry the wrong sign, all in the non-overlapping tails. Trim them and refit and $\hat\beta$ returns to $0.99$; reweight to the overlap instead — the `"ATO"` weights — and it returns $0.98$. Either way the coefficient had leaned on the extrapolation, and the diagnostic showed it, read off the regression itself with no separate model.

Whether it matters depends on the data. Here trimming moved $\hat\beta$ a lot, so the estimate leaned on the extrapolation. Had the outcome been linear — the two groups sharing one straight line — the same trim would barely move $\hat\beta$: the negative weights are still there, but dropping those units changes almost nothing. The check tells you which case you are in.

And it survives where the propensity score cannot. On the perfectly-separated data above the logistic model fails to converge and inverse-probability weighting collapses, yet the implied weights still flag every extrapolating unit.

## Out of OLS: the same weights beyond the linear model

The outcome reading has one advantage the effect reading lacks: it survives leaving OLS. Any estimator that can be written $\hat\tau = \sum_i \omega_i Y_i = \boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y$ — a linear combination of the observed outcomes — *has* outcome weights, whatever machinery produced them.

Knaus (2024) makes this the organising idea: augmented inverse-probability weighting, double machine learning, and the generalized random forest all admit a closed-form $\boldsymbol\omega$, obtained by the same residual-on-residual logic as OLS and computed off the shelf by his `OutcomeWeights` package. We derive it below; first, what it shows.

**Simulation — heterogeneous effects (DML).** Under a heterogeneous effect the two estimators diverge, and the weights show why. We simulate $n = 2000$ units: five standard-normal covariates $X$, selection $W_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(\Lambda(-1 + 1.2\,X_{i1}))$ with $\Lambda$ the logistic function (treatment is scarce and tied to $X_1$), and outcome $Y_i = \tau_i W_i + X_{i1} + 0.5\,X_{i2} + \varepsilon_i$ with an effect $\tau_i = 1 + X_{i1}$ that varies across units — a $W \times X_1$ interaction. The average effect is $E[\tau] = 1$, but units with larger $X_1$ have larger effects. `dml_with_smoother` returns a partially-linear (PLR) and an AIPW estimator; `get_outcome_weights` extracts the weight matrix $\boldsymbol\omega$, one row per estimator.

```{r}
#| message: false
#| warning: false
library(OutcomeWeights)
set.seed(1)
n <- 2000; p <- 5
X <- matrix(rnorm(n * p), n, p)
etrue <- plogis(-1 + 1.2 * X[, 1])                       # asymmetric selection on X1
W <- rbinom(n, 1, etrue)
tau <- 1 + X[, 1]                                        # heterogeneous effect: W x X1
Y <- tau * W + X[, 1] + 0.5 * X[, 2] + rnorm(n)
dml <- dml_with_smoother(Y, W, X, estimators = c("PLR", "AIPW_ATE"))
om  <- get_outcome_weights(dml)                          # om$omega: 2 x n, om$treat = W
invisible(capture.output(s <- summary(dml)))
W1 <- om$treat == 1
tab <- t(sapply(rownames(om$omega), function(nm) {
  w <- om$omega[nm, ]
  c(estimate = s[nm, 1], `omega %*% Y` = sum(w * Y),
    treated = sum(w[W1]), control = sum(w[!W1]))
}))
ov <- etrue * (1 - etrue)                                # e(1-e): PLR's per-unit weight
# ATE = plain mean of tau; overlap-ATE = e(1-e)-weighted mean of tau (PLR's target)
cat(sprintf("true ATE = %.2f   true overlap-ATE = %.2f\n", mean(tau), sum(ov * tau) / sum(ov)))
mtab(tab, 3)
```

Two things stand out. First, the estimators now *disagree* — PLR reports $1.29$, AIPW $1.07$ — and neither is wrong; they target different populations. PLR weights each unit by $e(X)(1 - e(X))$, the conditional variance of treatment — Angrist's propensity-variance weight from the previous chapter — so it leans on units where treatment is near a coin flip; its estimand is that $e(1-e)$-weighted average of the unit effects ($\approx 1.3$). AIPW instead reweights by $1/e$ and $1/(1-e)$ to undo selection, returning the plain average, the ATE ($\approx 1$). Same outcomes $\boldsymbol Y$, weighted two ways, two population-averages of $\tau$ — and the weights are what make the difference visible.

Second, the weights still reproduce each estimate exactly and sum to zero overall. They part on the other constraint from §1, $\sum_i \omega_i V_i = 1$: for a binary treatment that constraint is the treated weights summing to $+1$ (and, with the zero total, controls to $-1$). AIPW keeps it exactly; the partially-linear estimator sums to $\pm 0.99$, quietly breaking the within-group budget that OLS and AIPW hold.

That divergence shows what the representation is for. In Knaus's (2024) framing it does three things beyond producing the estimate. It puts OLS, IPW, AIPW, DML, and the causal forest on **one footing**, comparable through the single object $\boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y$. It **characterises the target population** each estimator reports — exactly the PLR-versus-AIPW gap above, two weightings of one set of outcomes. And it **checks covariate balance**, by handing $\boldsymbol\omega$ to `cobalt`, and flags when weights fail a property usually assumed — the within-group sum-to-$\pm 1$ that PLR breaks here.

The signed-extrapolation and leverage reading of §3 carries over too, but that is the `lmw` lineage the representation shares, not Knaus's own emphasis.

### The weights, derived

We have the OLS weights (@eq-omega); the same weights exist for the machine-learning estimators, derived the same way — residualise, then read off the coefficient on $\boldsymbol Y$. Take the partially-linear estimator (the R-learner behind the causal forest). It is FWL with the projections done by a flexible learner: residualise treatment and outcome on the covariates, then regress one residual on the other,

$$ \hat\tau = \frac{\sum_i (D_i - \hat e_i)(Y_i - \hat m_i)}{\sum_i (D_i - \hat e_i)^2}, $$

with $\hat e = \hat E[D \mid X]$ and $\hat m = \hat E[Y \mid X]$ the fitted nuisances.

The outcome nuisance is a *smoother*: its fitted values are a linear map of the training outcomes, $\hat{\boldsymbol m} = \boldsymbol S \boldsymbol Y$ — true for OLS, ridge, kernels, and honest forests alike. So the outcome residual is $\boldsymbol Y - \hat{\boldsymbol m} = (\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S)\boldsymbol Y$. Writing $\tilde{\boldsymbol D} = \boldsymbol D - \hat{\boldsymbol e}$ for the treatment residual and substituting,

$$ \hat\tau = \frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol D}'(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S)\,\boldsymbol Y}{\tilde{\boldsymbol D}'\tilde{\boldsymbol D}}. $$

Now $\boldsymbol Y$ appears once, linearly, in the numerator. Read off its coefficient and the weights are done:

$$ \hat\tau = \boldsymbol\omega'\boldsymbol Y, \qquad \boldsymbol\omega' = \frac{\tilde{\boldsymbol D}'(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S)}{\tilde{\boldsymbol D}'\tilde{\boldsymbol D}}. $$ {#eq-omega-ml}

That is @eq-omega — the OLS weights — with the projection annihilator $\boldsymbol M_X$ replaced by the smoother annihilator $\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S$. OLS is the case of a linear learner, $\boldsymbol S = \boldsymbol P_X$: there $\boldsymbol S$ is a projection, $\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S = \boldsymbol M_X$, and @eq-omega-ml is @eq-omega exactly.

Three moves did it: write the estimator, substitute the smoother $\hat{\boldsymbol m} = \boldsymbol S \boldsymbol Y$, read off the coefficient on $\boldsymbol Y$. They work for any estimator built from smoothers; only the operator on $\boldsymbol Y$ changes. AIPW is worth carrying through, because its operator is more than $\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S$.

*AIPW.* With outcome smoothers $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1 = \boldsymbol S_1 \boldsymbol Y$, $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_0 = \boldsymbol S_0 \boldsymbol Y$ and propensity $\hat{\boldsymbol e}$, the score of unit $i$ is

$$ \psi_i = \hat\mu_{1i} - \hat\mu_{0i} + \frac{D_i}{\hat e_i}\,(Y_i - \hat\mu_{1i}) - \frac{1 - D_i}{1 - \hat e_i}\,(Y_i - \hat\mu_{0i}), \qquad \hat\tau = \frac1n \sum_i \psi_i. $$

Each $\hat\mu_{ji}$ is $(\boldsymbol S_j \boldsymbol Y)_i$, so $\boldsymbol Y$ again enters only linearly; collecting its coefficient gives $\hat\tau = \boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y$ with

$$ \boldsymbol T = \boldsymbol S_1 - \boldsymbol S_0 + \operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\tfrac{\boldsymbol D}{\hat{\boldsymbol e}}\Big)(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S_1) - \operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\tfrac{1 - \boldsymbol D}{1 - \hat{\boldsymbol e}}\Big)(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S_0), \qquad \boldsymbol\omega' = \frac{\mathbf 1' \boldsymbol T}{n}. $$

These derived weights are exactly the rows of the table above. Multiply either $\boldsymbol\omega$ into $\boldsymbol Y$ and you recover that estimator's point estimate; sum them within the treated and control groups and you get the $\pm 1$ (AIPW) and $\pm 0.99$ (PLR) reported there. That is the use of deriving $\boldsymbol\omega$: once you have it, the reproduction check, the within-group budget, and the balance and overlap diagnostics all run on the same object, for any estimator built from smoothers.

The package does exactly this — reads $(\boldsymbol S, \boldsymbol S_0, \boldsymbol S_1, \hat{\boldsymbol e})$ from the fitted object, forms these operators, and reads off $\boldsymbol\omega$. The formulas above take a few lines to compute by hand; here we do it for both PLR and AIPW and check they match the package's weights:

```{r}
n  <- length(Y)
S  <- dml$NuPa.hat$smoothers$S[1, , ]         # outcome smoother E[Y|X]
S1 <- dml$NuPa.hat$smoothers$S.d1[1, , ]      # E[Y|X, D=1] smoother
S0 <- dml$NuPa.hat$smoothers$S.d0[1, , ]      # E[Y|X, D=0] smoother
e  <- dml$NuPa.hat$predictions$D.hat[, 1]     # fitted propensity

# PLR:  omega = (I - S)'(W - e) / ||W - e||^2
Wtil   <- W - e
om_plr <- as.numeric((Wtil - crossprod(S, Wtil)) / sum(Wtil^2))

# AIPW: omega = 1'T / n,  T = S1 - S0 + diag(W/e)(I - S1) - diag((1-W)/(1-e))(I - S0)
Imat    <- diag(n)
Tm      <- S1 - S0 + (W / e) * (Imat - S1) - ((1 - W) / (1 - e)) * (Imat - S0)
om_aipw <- colSums(Tm) / n

vtab(c(`PLR:  max |by hand - package|` = max(abs(om_plr  - om$omega["PLR", ])),
       `PLR:  omega' Y`                = sum(om_plr  * Y),
       `AIPW: max |by hand - package|` = max(abs(om_aipw - om$omega["AIPW-ATE", ])),
       `AIPW: omega' Y`                = sum(om_aipw * Y)), 4)
```

Agreement to machine precision: both by-hand $\boldsymbol\omega$ vectors match the package, and each $\boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y$ returns its point estimate — the derivation above, in code.

### Two uses: balance and extrapolation

Having $\boldsymbol\omega$, two checks run directly on it. Does the estimator's implied weighting balance the covariates? Hand $\boldsymbol\omega$ to `cobalt` and read the largest standardised mean difference. And is it extrapolating? Count the wrong-sign weights — a control with $\omega_i > 0$ or a treated unit with $\omega_i < 0$, the §3 signature of a comparison reaching off common support.

```{r}
#| message: false
#| warning: false
library(cobalt)
uses <- t(sapply(rownames(om$omega), function(nm) {
  w <- om$omega[nm, ]
  c(`max |SMD|`        = max(abs(bal.tab(as.data.frame(X), treat = W, weights = w)$Balance$Diff.Adj)),
    `wrong-sign units` = sum((om$treat == 1 & w < 0) | (om$treat == 0 & w > 0)))
}))
mtab(uses, 3)
```

Raw, the groups are badly imbalanced — the largest covariate SMD is $1.11$, since treatment was tied to $X_1$. Both weightings pull it down to near zero ($0.03$ for PLR, $0.09$ for AIPW): each balances the covariates, which for a causal forest you could not otherwise check.

They differ on extrapolation. PLR carries 21 wrong-sign controls — it leans slightly off common support — while AIPW carries none. Neither is disqualifying here; the point is that balance and extrapolation are now *checkable*, on estimators that never exposed a weighting.

## From two dots to four

The two-dot construction scales with how saturated the design is: each cell the regressors carve out collapses to its mean, and $\hat\beta$ is a signed contrast of those cell means. Two cases make it concrete.

**Simulation — treatment alone.** Regress $y$ on the treatment $D$ alone — here 30 treated drawn $y \sim N(5, 1)$ and 50 controls $y \sim N(2, 1)$. The residual $\tilde V_i = D_i - \bar D$ is constant within each group, so the $|d|$-weighting is uniform there and the two dots are simply the group means: $\hat\beta = \bar y_1 - \bar y_0$, the difference in means. Every treated unit then carries positive weight, every control negative — the structural pattern of the previous section, with the wrong-sign off-diagonal empty until covariates enter.

```{r}
#| fig-width: 5
#| fig-height: 4
#| fig-cap: "Regression on the treatment alone. Points are the two groups (rust control, teal treated); the large dots are the group means, and the segment joining them has slope $\\hat\\beta = \\bar y_1 - \\bar y_0$, the difference in means."
set.seed(7)
n1 <- 30; n0 <- 50
d <- c(rep(1, n1), rep(0, n0)); y <- c(rnorm(n1, 5), rnorm(n0, 2))
dev <- d - mean(d); om <- dev / sum(dev^2); col <- ifelse(om >= 0, teal, rust)
y1 <- mean(y[d == 1]); y0 <- mean(y[d == 0])
plot(jitter(d, 0.4), y, pch = 16, col = paste0(col, "55"), cex = 0.9,
     xlim = c(-0.3, 1.3), xaxt = "n", xlab = "", ylab = "y", main = "Regression on treatment")
axis(1, at = c(0, 1), labels = c("control", "treated"))
segments(0, y0, 1, y1, lwd = 2.6, col = "grey15")
points(c(0, 1), c(y0, y1), pch = 21, bg = c(rust, teal), col = "white", cex = 2.5, lwd = 2)
vtab(c(`beta (lm)` = coef(lm(y ~ d))[["d"]], `y1 - y0` = y1 - y0,
        `off-diagonal weights` = sum((d == 1 & om < 0) | (d == 0 & om > 0))), 3)
```

The estimate equals $\bar y_1 - \bar y_0$, and no weight carries the wrong sign — the off-diagonal count is zero.

**Simulation — 2×2 difference-in-differences.** Add a period and interact it with the group, and the design has four cells. Each collapses to its mean, and the interaction coefficient is their double contrast,

$$ \widehat{\text{DiD}} = (\bar y_{T,\text{post}} - \bar y_{T,\text{pre}}) - (\bar y_{C,\text{post}} - \bar y_{C,\text{pre}}). $$

Two dots have become four, and with them four slopes — the two group trends over time and the two cross-sectional gaps. The DiD is the difference between either parallel pair, trend minus trend or gap minus gap. We simulate 200 units in each group×period cell from $y = 3 + 2g + p + 2\,(g \cdot p) + \varepsilon$, so the true DiD (the interaction) is $2$:

```{r}
#| fig-width: 5
#| fig-height: 4
#| fig-cap: "The 2×2 difference-in-differences: four cell means (dots), the treated (teal) and control (rust) trends, the parallel-trends counterfactual (dashed), and the DiD (black bar) as the gap between the treated post-mean and that counterfactual."
set.seed(3)
g <- rep(c(1, 1, 0, 0), each = 200); p <- rep(c(0, 1, 0, 1), each = 200)
y <- 3 + 2 * g + 1 * p + 2 * (g * p) + rnorm(800)
m <- tapply(y, list(g, p), mean)
Tpre <- m["1", "0"]; Tpost <- m["1", "1"]; Cpre <- m["0", "0"]; Cpost <- m["0", "1"]
cf <- Tpre + (Cpost - Cpre)
plot(NA, xlim = c(-0.15, 1.25), ylim = range(y), xaxt = "n", xlab = "", ylab = "y",
     main = "Difference-in-differences")
axis(1, at = c(0, 1), labels = c("pre", "post"))
segments(0, Tpre, 1, Tpost, lwd = 2.4, col = teal)
segments(0, Cpre, 1, Cpost, lwd = 2.4, col = rust)
segments(0, Tpre, 1, cf, lty = 2, lwd = 1.6, col = "grey55")
segments(1, cf, 1, Tpost, lwd = 4, col = "grey15")
points(c(0, 1, 0, 1), c(Tpre, Tpost, Cpre, Cpost),
       pch = 21, bg = c(teal, teal, rust, rust), col = "white", cex = 2.3, lwd = 2)
text(1.02, (cf + Tpost) / 2, "DiD", pos = 4, cex = 0.9, col = "grey15")
vtab(c(`treated trend` = Tpost - Tpre, `control trend` = Cpost - Cpre,
        `pre gap` = Tpre - Cpre, `post gap` = Tpost - Cpost,
        `DiD (interaction)` = coef(lm(y ~ g * p))[["g:p"]]), 3)
```

The estimated interaction recovers the true DiD of $2$, and the four cell-mean weights carry the structural signs $(+, -, -, +)$ as long as every cell is populated.

The clean $2 \times 2$ has trivial weights — four cell means, each $\pm 1$. Real difference-in-differences carries covariates: parallel trends holds only *conditional* on $X$, and the comparison has to be modelled. That model is a weighting of outcomes too.

The doubly-robust DiD of Sant'Anna and Zhao (2020) is AIPW read on the outcome *change* $\Delta Y = Y_1 - Y_0$: a propensity for treatment, a control-group regression of $\Delta Y$ on $X$, combined. By the recipe of the last section its estimate is $\widehat{\text{ATT}} = \boldsymbol\omega' \Delta Y$, with $\boldsymbol\omega' = \boldsymbol a'(\mathbf I - \boldsymbol S)$ — the control-trend smoother $\boldsymbol S$ and the group weights $\boldsymbol a = \boldsymbol w_{\text{treat}} - \boldsymbol w_{\text{cont}}$.

**Simulation — doubly-robust DiD.** Two periods and $n = 2000$: treatment is selected on $x_1$ with thin overlap, $\Pr(D = 1) = \Lambda(-0.5 + 2 x_1)$; the control trend depends on $x_1$, so trends are parallel only given $X$; the true ATT is $1$. We fit DR-DiD with `DRDID`, rebuild its weights by hand, and check.

```{r}
#| message: false
#| warning: false
library(DRDID)
set.seed(2)
n  <- 2000
x1 <- rnorm(n); x2 <- rnorm(n); Xcov <- cbind(1, x1, x2)
D  <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.5 + 2 * x1))        # selection into treatment on x1 (thin overlap)
y0 <- 1 + x1 + 0.5 * x2 + rnorm(n)               # pre-period outcome
y1 <- y0 + (0.5 + 0.4 * x1) + 1 * D + rnorm(n)   # control trend depends on x1; true ATT = 1
dY <- y1 - y0
att <- drdid_panel(y1 = y1, y0 = y0, D = D, covariates = Xcov, boot = FALSE)$ATT

# rebuild the DR-DiD weights: omega = (I - S)' a, acting on the outcome change dY
e    <- pmin(as.vector(glm(D ~ x1 + x2, family = binomial)$fitted), 1 - 1e-6)
keep <- rep(TRUE, n); keep[D == 0] <- e[D == 0] < 0.995     # DRDID's control trimming
Xc   <- Xcov[D == 0, ]
S    <- matrix(0, n, n); S[, D == 0] <- Xcov %*% solve(crossprod(Xc)) %*% t(Xc)
w.t  <- keep * D
w.c  <- keep * e * (1 - D) / (1 - e)
a    <- w.t / sum(w.t) - w.c / sum(w.c)          # group weights, treated minus reweighted control
omega <- as.vector(a - crossprod(S, a))

Xk <- cbind(x1, x2); sds <- apply(Xk, 2, sd)
vtab(c(`DRDID ATT`                = att,
       `omega' dY`                = sum(omega * dY),
       `wrong-sign weights`       = sum((D == 1 & omega < 0) | (D == 0 & omega > 0)),
       `covariate imbalance, raw` = max(abs((colMeans(Xk[D == 1, ]) - colMeans(Xk[D == 0, ])) / sds)),
       `covariate imbalance, wtd` = max(abs(as.vector(a %*% Xk) / sds))), 3)
```

$\boldsymbol\omega' \Delta Y$ reproduces the `DRDID` estimate exactly — the difference-in-differences is a signed weighting of observed outcome *changes*, and the diagnostics of this chapter apply to it. The weighting balances the covariate that drove selection (its standardised imbalance falls from $1.20$ to $0.09$). And $151$ units carry a wrong-sign weight — a treated unit subtracted, or a control added — the §3 signature of a comparison reaching past common support.

Negative weights return in *staggered* designs one level up — among the comparisons rather than the units. Two-way fixed-effects DiD averages many such $2 \times 2$'s, and some enter with a **negative** weight: the forbidden comparisons that use already-treated units as controls (de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille 2020; Goodman-Bacon 2021). Those weight the treatment *effects*, not the outcomes — the effect-weight cousin — but the reading is the one just seen: a comparison entering with a sign it should not, because the design has run out of clean controls.

A closing caution. These weights diagnose *estimation* — which comparisons a difference-in-differences rests on, and whether it leans on non-overlapping or forbidden ones — not *identification*.

Parallel trends is an assumption about the treated group's unobserved untreated path, and no weighting of observed outcomes can test it: a negative weight and a failure of parallel trends are independent, either able to occur without the other.

Pre-trends are probed separately, by the event-study leads. And weights *chosen* to match the pre-treatment paths — synthetic difference-in-differences and its kin — are a different object from the ones implied here.

## Summary

1. **The second reading.** FWL writes $\hat\beta = \sum_i \omega_i y_i$ with $\omega_i = \tilde V_i / \sum_j \tilde V_j^2$ (@eq-omega): a signed, computable weighting of the *observed* outcomes, distinct from the effect weights $w_i = \tilde V_i V_i$.
2. **Two constraints.** The outcome weights sum to zero and the weighted regressor to one: $\sum_i \omega_i = 0$, $\sum_i \omega_i V_i = 1$.
3. **Signed, not squared.** Because $\omega_i \propto \tilde V_i$ rather than $\tilde V_i^2$, it reveals *direction*: which rows push $\hat\beta$ up, which down, and — under controls with thin overlap — which enter with a sign their raw position would not predict.
4. **It leaves OLS.** Any $\hat\tau = \boldsymbol\omega' \boldsymbol Y$ has outcome weights; the same object and its diagnostics extend to AIPW, DML, and causal forests (Knaus 2024), with the caveat that the tidy sum-to-zero of OLS need not become sum-to-$\pm 1$ within groups.
5. **Saturation scales it.** Regressed on the treatment alone, $\hat\beta$ is the difference in group means — two dots; a $2 \times 2$ difference-in-differences is a signed contrast of four cell means — four dots. With covariates a doubly-robust DiD is a weighting of the outcome *changes*, $\widehat{\text{ATT}} = \boldsymbol\omega' \Delta Y$ (Sant'Anna and Zhao 2020), open to the same diagnostics; negative weights return in staggered designs as forbidden comparisons (de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille 2020).
