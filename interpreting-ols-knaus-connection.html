Interpreting OLS  ·  a connection note  ·  outcome weights  /  effect weights

Two readings of one coefficient

The chapter Interpreting OLS already gives the weighted-outcome reading of a coefficient — it just goes no further with it. Knaus’s Treatment Effect Estimators as Weighted Outcomes (2024) picks up that exact thread and carries it past OLS. A handoff on one Frisch–Waugh–Lovell identity.

β̂ = ∑i Ṽi yi / ∑j Ṽj2
One FWL coefficient
Weights on outcomes
β̂ = ∑i ωi yi

Signed, sum to zero, computable — because y is observed. Holds always: it is algebra.

Weights on effects
β̂ = ∑i wi βi / ∑i wi

Non-negative, not computable — the per-unit βi are never seen. Holds only in expectation.

Both readings are the chapter’s. It develops the effect reading in full; it defines the outcome reading and goes no further — which is exactly where Knaus picks up.

01The same identity, split two ways

Both papers start from Frisch–Waugh–Lovell. The chapter reads it twice; Knaus builds his whole framework on the first reading.

The chapter’s central move (its section The general rule: FWL) is that a single OLS coefficient admits two readings. Write Ṽi for the regressor of interest after the controls are residualized out. Then

β̂ = ∑i ωi yi,    ωi = Ṽi / ∑j Ṽj2 weights on outcomes — which rows drive the number
β̂ = (∑i wi βi) / ∑i wi,    wi = Ṽi Vi weights on effects — whose effect is being estimated

Knaus’s entire paper is about the first object. His Equation (1), τ̂ = ∑i ωi Yi = ω′Y, is precisely the chapter’s outcome-weight reading. And his running OLS example derives the closed form

ωols′ = (Ê′Ê)−1 Ê′ MX,    Ê = MX D Knaus, Eq. 7

which is the chapter’s ωi: the treatment residual Ê is the chapter’s Ṽ, and applying the residual-maker MX to Y is just the FWL residualizing of the outcome. The chapter’s outcome-weight formula is the base case of Knaus’s framework.

02The same distinction, drawn by both

The chapter’s comparison table and Knaus’s taxonomy are the same two columns under two names.

Knaus separates outcome weights from effect weights in almost the chapter’s words: outcome weights “apply to observed outcomes and numerically reproduce the estimate without further assumptions, while [effect] weights weight inherently unobservable effects and usually reproduce the estimate only in expectation.” Line up the two vocabularies and they coincide exactly.

The mnemonic runs through the chapter’s own figures: the rust rays that draw the weights on the sample geometry are the effect weights; the teal fit line they average into is the outcome-weight combination. The two colors on this page carry the same code.

03Where the chapter stops, Knaus continues

Not opposite directions — the same thread. The chapter defines the outcome-weight reading and sets it down after one step; Knaus is the rest of that walk.

The chapter runs two threads. It develops the effect-weight reading in full — Angrist, Słoczyński, within vs. between — all inside OLS, fixed effects, and panels, where that reading naturally lives. It also states the outcome-weight reading: it defines ωi, gives it one diagnostic use (overlap in doubt, wrong-sign rows), and then goes no further — “default to the effect reading,” it says. Knaus takes that same object and carries it past OLS.

Effect weights wi
Chapter defines → Angrist 1998 → Słoczyński 2022 → Hazlett & Shinkre — developed in full, stays in OLS & panels
Outcome weights ωi
Chapter defines + one use chapter stops · Knaus continues DML → Causal & instrumental forests → AIPW · TSLS · IPW

So the join is a handoff, not a contrast. The chapter’s ωi is Knaus’s base case, and his framework is the continuation the chapter names but does not pursue — the same outcome-weight object, now computed for a causal forest, an AIPW estimator, DML, and the rest through a single transformation matrix. The effect-weight thread, by contrast, is one the chapter follows to the end and Knaus deliberately brackets as separate.

04The practical bridge

If you ever want the chapter’s outcome reading for something richer than plain OLS, Knaus hands you the tool.

The chapter recommends the outcome reading precisely when overlap is in doubt and rows may enter with the wrong sign — a diagnostic the squared effect weights cannot reveal. That is exactly Knaus’s motivating use-case list: covariate-balance checks, extrapolation diagnostics for negative weights, target-population characterization.

Off the shelf Knaus’s R package OutcomeWeights computes the signed ωi for a causal forest, an AIPW estimator, DML, and the rest — the same object the chapter defines for OLS, now available beyond the linear model. The chapter defines the object; Knaus operationalizes it.

05One caveat where they connect

The chapter’s clean “sum to zero” is exactly what stops being clean past OLS.

The chapter notes the outcome weights ωi sum to zero — a tidy algebraic fact for OLS. Knaus’s headline empirical finding is that partially-linear-regression-based estimators, such as causal forests, produce outcome weights that sum to zero overall but not to (minus) one within the treated and untreated groups — a property often assumed desirable. That subtlety only surfaces once you leave OLS, where the chapter’s clean statement lives. The chapter’s base case is where Knaus’s complication has not yet appeared.
